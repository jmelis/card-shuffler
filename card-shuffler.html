How to do it

P piles in a row, left to right, same spots every pass.

  1. Deal from the top of the deck, face‑down, one card per number.
  2. Stack pile 1 onto 2, that onto 3, and so on rightwards. Pile 1 ends on top.
  3. Next pass, repeat.
Never flip or reorder a pile, and always stack low to high. Those two rules are the shuffle.

Misdeal, dropped card, lost your place? Restart.

Why this works

The idea in one sentence

Give every card a secret random number, then sort the deck by those numbers. If the numbers were handed out at random, the sorted order is random.

The keys

Each card gets its own number, its key. \(k(c)\) denotes the key of card \(c\): if the third card gets the number 37, then \(k(3) = 37\).

The keys have two properties:

  • Distinct — no two cards get the same number. Such a map is called an injection, or one-to-one.
  • Uniform — every possible way of handing out distinct numbers is exactly as likely as every other.

Together: a uniform random injection from cards to keys.

Sorting the deck by hand

Sorting 52 cards into numerical order directly would be miserable, so the deck is sorted the way a computer sorts digit strings — a radix sort, one digit at a time.

Write each key in base \(P\), where \(P\) is your number of piles. With \(P = 8\) and two digits, the key 37 is \(45_8\) — digits 5 (units) and 4 (eights). Pass \(i\) sends card \(c\) to the pile given by its \(i\)-th digit:

$$\text{pile} \;=\; \left\lfloor \frac{k(c)}{P^{\,i}} \right\rfloor \bmod P$$

starting with \(i = 0\), the last digit, and working up. Each pass sorts the deck by one digit without destroying the previous pass's work: cards sharing the current digit land in the same pile and keep the order they already had, which was sorted by the earlier digits. After \(M\) passes the deck is in full key order.

Hence the two rules — every pile handled identically, piles collected in fixed ascending order. They are the sort.

Dealing face‑down leaves each pile internally reversed rather than order-preserving. This is still exact: reversing every pile at every pass sorts the deck by a relabelled key. With \(M = 2\) the final order is sorted by

$$f(k) \;=\; d_1 \cdot P + (P - 1 - d_0)$$

where \(d_0, d_1\) are the key's digits. Relabelling is a bijection on the key space, so distinct keys stay distinct and uniform stays uniform. Dealing face‑up and flipping each pile is the order-preserving alternative, equally exact. Mixing the two inside one shuffle is what breaks the guarantee.

How many passes

\(M\) digits in base \(P\) express \(P^M\) different keys, and at least \(N\) are needed to give every card a distinct one:

$$P^M \ge N \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad M = \left\lceil \log_P N \right\rceil$$

For a 52-card deck with 8 piles, \(8^2 = 64 \ge 52\), so \(M = 2\). Two passes.

Exact uniformity

A shuffle is fair if all \(N!\) orderings of the deck are equally likely. For 52 cards:

$$52! \approx 8.07 \times 10^{67} \text{ orderings, each with probability } \tfrac{1}{52!}$$

Most real shuffling only gets close, and how close takes a calculation to establish. The standard comparison is the riffle shuffle, the casino move where the deck is split in two and the halves interleaved by releasing cards alternately from each thumb. One riffle barely disturbs a deck; around seven are needed for 52 cards, and even then some orderings remain measurably more likely than others. That residue is correlation with the order the deck was already in — precisely what a shuffle exists to destroy.

The radix method leaves no residue. Fix the set of \(N\) key values that happened to get used. Whatever that set is, the \(N!\) ways of matching cards to those values are equally likely, and each matching sorts into a different final ordering. Matchings therefore correspond one-to-one with orderings, and

$$\Pr[\text{any particular final order}] = \frac{1}{N!} \quad\text{exactly}$$

independent of the starting order, with no approximation and no mixing-time argument. The guarantee is conditional only on the deal being executed correctly.

Where the randomness comes from

The keys are drawn from your browser's cryptographic generator (crypto.getRandomValues), not Math.random, and converted to a range by rejection sampling so there is no modulo bias. The assignment itself uses a partial Fisher–Yates shuffle, which is uniform over injections by construction.

Self test

Runs the app's actual dealing logic on a small deck many times and compares the distribution over all \(N!\) outcomes against uniform with a \(\chi^2\) test, for both the face‑down and face‑up conventions. A correct implementation lands \(\chi^2\) near its degrees of freedom.
